# Pricing Elasticity & Revenue Intelligence System

A development-ready data science project analyzing pricing, discounts, sales
volume, revenue, competitor pricing, and marketing spend across a synthetic
retail transactions dataset — including price-elasticity estimation, OLS
statistical modeling, and a demand-prediction linear regression model.

---

## Project Overview

This project builds an end-to-end pricing analytics pipeline: synthetic data
generation → validation → cleaning → EDA → statistical analysis → price
elasticity estimation → machine learning demand model → browser-based
dashboards. It is designed to be explored locally, understood line-by-line, and
discussed confidently in a data science interview.

## Business Problem

Retail and e-commerce pricing teams routinely ask:

- How does price relate to quantity sold?
- How do discounts drive demand, and at what cost to margin?
- Does pricing/discounting behavior differ meaningfully by product, category,
  region, or customer segment?
- Which products/categories/regions drive the most revenue?
- Can historical data support even a simple demand forecast?

## Why This Problem Matters

Pricing is one of the highest-leverage levers a business controls — a small
misstep in discount strategy or category pricing can meaningfully impact
margin. Understanding price sensitivity (elasticity) by segment lets a
business target discounts where they're effective and avoid unnecessary
margin erosion where demand is inelastic.

## Dataset

A **synthetic** dataset generated by `src/generate_data.py` (~55,000 rows,
fixed random seed = reproducible). It simulates:

- 6 product categories, ~47 products, 5 regions, ~19 stores
- Seasonality (yearly cycle + holiday/weekend boosts)
- Realistic **missing values** (price, discount, competitor price, marketing
  spend), **duplicate rows**, **outliers** (bulk-order style quantity spikes),
  and a few **impossible values** (negative prices from data-entry errors)
- A believable, non-trivial price-elasticity structure built into the demand
  simulation (so recovered elasticities can be sanity-checked)

Columns: `transaction_id, date, product_id, product_name, category, region,
store_id, quantity_sold, unit_price, discount_percentage, revenue,
customer_segment, competitor_price, marketing_spend`

> The raw/processed CSVs are **not** committed to source control (see
> `.gitignore`) — regenerate them locally with the commands below.

## Data Preprocessing

Documented in `src/data_cleaning.py`:
- Exact duplicate rows dropped.
- Negative `unit_price` values (data-entry errors) corrected via absolute
  value; rows still missing price are **dropped** (price is central to this
  analysis, so imputing it would bias elasticity estimates).
- Missing `discount_percentage` treated as "no discount" (0).
- Missing `competitor_price` / `marketing_spend` imputed with the
  **category-level median**.
- Extreme quantity outliers (top/bottom 0.1%) are **winsorized**, not
  dropped, since bulk orders are plausible but shouldn't dominate a linear
  model.
- `revenue` is recomputed after cleaning for internal consistency.

## EDA

`src/eda.py` produces 6 charts into `visualizations/`: revenue by category,
monthly revenue trend, price vs. quantity (scatter), discount distribution,
revenue by region, and average revenue per transaction by segment.

## Statistical Analysis

`src/statistical_analysis.py` uses `statsmodels` OLS to quantify:
`price -> quantity`, `discount -> quantity`, `price -> revenue`, and
`marketing spend -> revenue`, reporting coefficients, standard errors,
p-values, confidence intervals, and R² / adjusted R² for each model.

**These are observational associations, not proven causal effects** — see
Limitations.

### Price Elasticity

`src/elasticity_analysis.py` estimates elasticity via a log-log OLS
(`log(quantity) ~ log(price) + discount`), broken out by product, category,
region, and customer segment. It deliberately reports **two versions**:

1. A naive group-level estimate.
2. A version with **product fixed effects**, which controls for different
   products in the same group having different base prices/demand. The
   naive estimates can come out with an unexpected sign due to this
   confounding — the fixed-effects version corrects for it. This is a
   worked example of why raw cross-sectional comparisons can mislead.

## Machine Learning

`src/modeling.py` trains a **Linear Regression** model (scikit-learn) to
predict `quantity_sold` from price, discount, marketing spend, competitor
price, and categorical features (one-hot encoded). Uses a **time-based**
train/test split (train on earlier dates, test on later ones) since that
mirrors real forecasting use. Compared against a naive baseline (predicting
the training-set mean).

## Model Evaluation

Reported with **MAE, RMSE, and R²** (never accuracy — this is a regression
problem), plus a residual distribution plot and an actual-vs-predicted
scatter plot. Exact numbers will be generated when you run the notebook —
see "How to Run" below. Expect a **modest R²**: demand here includes Poisson
noise and outliers, so a linear model is not expected to explain all
variance; a modest, well-behaved model is more trustworthy than a suspicious
near-perfect fit.

## Key Findings

Results are generated locally when you run the notebook (the dataset is
regenerated per-machine from a fixed seed, so results are consistent). The
notebook's Section 12 ("Business Interpretation") walks through:
1. Discount depth reliably increases unit volume, at a revenue-per-unit
   trade-off.
2. Fixed-effects elasticity estimates are negative and economically sensible
   across every category/region/segment.
3. Revenue is meaningfully seasonal (Nov/Dec uplift).
4. The linear demand model improves on a naive baseline.

## Limitations

- **Synthetic data**: absolute numbers are illustrative, not real-world
  figures. The methodology, not the specific coefficients, is the
  transferable takeaway.
- **Observational, not experimental**: price variation was not randomly
  assigned, so even the fixed-effects elasticity estimates are statistical
  associations, not proven causal effects. A true causal estimate would
  require a randomized price test or a quasi-experimental method
  (instrumental variables, regression discontinuity, diff-in-diff).
- Linear regression assumes linear relationships and won't capture
  non-linearities or interaction effects (e.g. diminishing marketing
  returns).
- Outliers were winsorized rather than modeled explicitly.

## Future Improvements

- Add regularized regression (Ridge/Lasso) or gradient-boosted trees for
  comparison against the linear baseline.
- Model outliers/bulk orders as a separate segment.
- Extend elasticity analysis with a proper panel/fixed-effects library
  (e.g. `linearmodels`) for standard-error clustering by product.
- Build an actual A/B price test to validate the elasticity estimates
  causally (see the companion `Experimentation_AB_Testing_Lab` project).

## Interview Questions

1. **Why did you build this project?**
   To demonstrate an end-to-end pricing analytics workflow: from messy raw
   data to a statistically grounded, business-interpretable set of findings.
2. **Why generate synthetic data instead of using a public dataset?**
   It allows control over realistic data-quality issues (missing values,
   outliers, duplicates) and lets me embed a known elasticity structure to
   sanity-check whether the analysis correctly recovers it.
3. **How did you handle missing values, and why?**
   Column-specific: price-missing rows are dropped (price is central to the
   analysis), discount-missing is treated as zero, competitor price/
   marketing spend are imputed with the category median — each decision is
   documented in `data_cleaning.py` rather than applied blindly.
4. **Why drop rows with negative quantity instead of using them?**
   Negative sold quantity is not physically meaningful in this context (as
   opposed to negative price, which is plausibly a sign error); dropping is
   safer than guessing which sign was intended.
5. **What's a winsorization, and why use it instead of dropping outliers?**
   Winsorizing caps extreme values at a percentile threshold rather than
   removing them, preserving the sample size while limiting the influence
   of implausibly large values on a linear model.
6. **What does the log-log elasticity specification actually estimate?**
   The coefficient on `log(price)` approximates the % change in quantity for
   a 1% change in price, holding included controls fixed — the standard
   definition of price elasticity of demand.
7. **Why did the naive category-level elasticity come out positive/wrong-signed?**
   Because it mixes within-product price variation with between-product
   differences in base price and base demand — a Simpson's-paradox-style
   confound. Adding product fixed effects isolates the within-product
   relationship, which is what price elasticity is actually meant to
   capture.
8. **Why use a time-based split instead of a random split for the ML model?**
   Because in a real deployment the model would predict future demand from
   past data — a random split would leak future information into training
   and overstate performance.
9. **Why Linear Regression instead of a more complex model?**
   It's interpretable (coefficients have direct business meaning), fast to
   train, and a reasonable baseline; the README's "Future Improvements"
   notes where a more flexible model could help.
10. **Why MAE and RMSE instead of accuracy?**
    `quantity_sold` is continuous — accuracy is a classification metric and
    doesn't apply. RMSE penalizes large errors more than MAE; reporting both
    gives a fuller picture.
11. **What would you tell a stakeholder who says "just cut prices to sell more"?**
    Cutting price only helps revenue where demand is sufficiently elastic
    (elasticity below -1) that the volume gain outweighs the per-unit
    revenue loss; the segment/category breakdown identifies where that's
    actually true versus not.
12. **What's the difference between correlation, association, and causation
    in this project?**
    Correlation/association describe patterns in the observed data;
    causation requires ruling out confounding and reverse causality, usually
    via randomization or a valid quasi-experimental design — which this
    observational dataset does not provide.
13. **How would you validate these findings before acting on them?**
    Run a small randomized price/discount test on a subset of
    products/regions and compare the observed effect to the elasticity
    estimate from this analysis.
14. **What's a limitation of using category-level medians to impute missing
    competitor price?**
    It assumes missingness is unrelated to the true competitor price within
    a category (a "missing at random" assumption), which may not hold if,
    say, missing competitor prices are more common for niche
    hard-to-track products.
15. **How would you extend this to a real production setting** (even though
    this project is intentionally development-ready, not production-ready)?
    Automate data refresh, add monitoring for data drift/elasticity drift
    over time, and validate the demand model against holdout time periods
    on a rolling basis.
